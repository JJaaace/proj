Language Python 3.11+
One-line
pitch
An AI-powered assistant that texts electric utility customers timely, personalized, plain-
language updates before, during and after a major storm outage.
The problem
When a major storm approaches, utility customers want three answers: Will my power go out?
Why is it out? When will it come back? Today they usually get generic messages, wrong
restoration times, or nothing at all, so they ﬂood call centers. Many confusing situations (for
example, "my neighbor has power but I don't") are never explained.
Our solution
A pipeline that combines ﬁve kinds of intelligence and turns them into one accurate, personal
text message per customer:
1. Predictive analytics: who is likely to lose power, and when it will likely be restored.
2. Geospatial context: where the storm hits and when it reaches each customer.
3. Network relationships: how the grid is wired, so we know exactly who shares a failed
device.
4. Operational restoration inputs: what crews are actually doing in real time.
5. Customer communication design: how to say it clearly, honestly and safely.
--- PAGE 2 ---
Core design principle
The AI never invents facts about the grid. Models and data compute the truth. The
messaging layer only explains it. If we don't know a restoration time, the message says so
honestly instead of guessing.
2. Goals and non-goals
Goals (what we must demo)
1. A simulated storm moves through a ﬁctional town over simulated time.
2. Before the storm, at-risk customers get a pre-storm warning text.
3. As equipment fails, exactly the right customers (traced through the grid graph) get an
outage text explaining the cause.
4. As crews are assigned and work, customers get updates with an estimated restoration
time range and conﬁdence level.
5. When power returns, customers get a restored text.
6. At least one message is delivered to a real phone during the demo (via Twilio trial).
7. Customers with medical equipment are ﬂagged and prioritized.
Stretch goals (only after the goals above work)
Two-way texting: customer replies STATUS  and gets their current snapshot.
An AI language model personalizes message wording (still grounded in the snapshot).
A simple web outage map or internal dashboard.
Spanish-language messages.
Non-goals (do NOT build these)
Connections to real utility systems (real OMS, SCADA, smart meters). We use synthetic
data only.
Web app or voice/phone channels for customers (folders exist as placeholders for the
future).
User accounts, login, or payment of any kind.
Production deployment, scaling, or security hardening.
Real customer personal data. All names and phone numbers are fake, except the team's
own veriﬁed demo phones.

4. The ﬁve components explained
4.1 Predictive analytics (Person 2)
Outage risk (before the storm): a score from 0 to 1 per feeder estimating the chance of an
outage. Inputs: forecast wind gust, rainfall, vegetation density, asset age, overhead vs.
underground lines. A simple weighted formula or small scikit-learn model on synthetic
data is acceptable.
Estimated time of restoration, ETR (during the storm): given damage type, crews
available and current time, return an earliest time, latest time and conﬁdence (low ,
medium , high ). Never a single exact time.

4.2 Geospatial context (Person 2)
Model the storm as a center point moving along a straight path at a ﬁxed speed with a
wind radius.
Compute when the storm reaches each customer and whether they're in the high-wind
zone.
Keep it simple: plain math on latitude/longitude (haversine distance) is ﬁne. Shapely is
optional.
4.3 Network relationships (Person 1)
The grid is a directed graph: substation → feeder → transformer → customer .
Key insight: physical closeness is not electrical connection. Neighbors on different
transformers can have different outcomes, and the graph explains why.
Must support: ﬁnding all customers downstream of a failed device, ﬁnding the path
upstream from a customer, and guessing the root-cause device from a group of outaged
customers.
4.4 Operational restoration inputs (Person 1 creates data, Person 3 consumes it)
In real life these come from outage systems, smart meters and crew dispatch. For us they
come from a storm timeline ﬁle: a scripted list of events (device fails, crew assigned,
crew on site, device restored) at simulated times.
These events always override predictions. Once a device is actually reported as restored,
it's restored, no matter what a model said.
4.5 Customer communication design (Person 4)
Plain language at about an 8th-grade reading level. "A tree fell on a line serving your
street," not "fault on lateral L-1203."
One SMS segment: 160 characters max per message.
Honest uncertainty: if there's no ETR, say crews are assessing and when the next update
will come.
Proactive: send at meaningful moments only (see the trigger rules in Section 7).
Safety wording (downed wires, gas smell, call 911) is ﬁxed text, never generated or edited
by AI.
Every message ends with opt-out info where required: Reply STOP to opt out  (can be
shortened to STOP=end ).

Throttle: at most one message per customer per 30 simulated minutes, exceptrestored ,
which always sends. If throttled, the latest information goes in the next allowed message.
Medical priority customers get every alert type with no throttling, and their
outage_confirmed  message includes a line pointing to emergency resources.

9. The demo (Person 3 leads, everyone supports)
Run python -m tests.storm_replay.replay  (dry-run by default; --live  to send real texts to
veriﬁed team phones only).
The replay steps simulated time forward (e.g., 10 minutes per step), and at each step:
1. Applies any storm_timeline.json  events up to the current time.
2. Updates storm position and risk (Person 2).
3. Traces affected customers through the graph (Person 1).
4. Rebuilds snapshots and runs triggers (Person 3).
5. Sends each alert through handle_alert  (Person 4).
6. Prints a readable log: time, storm position, outages, and each message sent.
Demo story (target, about 3 minutes): storm approaches → pre-storm warnings → tree takes
out a transformer → those customers get outage texts while a neighbor on another transformer
stays powered (show the graph explaining why) → crew assigned → ETR updates as damage is
assessed → medical customer prioritized → restored texts arrive, one on a real phone in front of
the judges.

12. Glossary
Term Meaning
Substation Facility that steps down high-voltage power and feeds several feeders.
Feeder A main distribution circuit leaving a substation, serving a neighborhood.
Transformer Device on a pole or pad that serves a handful of homes on one street.
Downstream Everything fed by a device. If a device fails, everything downstream loses power.
ETR Estimated time of restoration. We always express it as a range plus conﬁdence.
OMS Outage management system, the utility's real-time outage tracker (simulated by our
timeline).
AMI / smart
meter
Meters that report when power drops or returns (simulated by our timeline).
Snapshot Our per-customer record of current status, cause, ETR and crew status (Section 6.4).
Dry-run Messages are printed instead of actually sent.
Medical priority Customer depends on powered medical equipment; gets extra alerts and outreach.
